How to Avoid Harmful Guest Posts

The wrong backlink doesn't just waste money, it can drag your rankings down. Here's how to tell a real placement from a toxic one, before you pay.

By the Freddy team · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read

Most people assume any dofollow link helps. It doesn't. A link from the wrong site can be neutral, wasted, or actively harmful, and cheap guest posts are where the damage usually starts. This guide shows you exactly what to look for.

What makes a guest post "harmful"

Google's guidelines treat links that exist mainly to manipulate rankings as spam. The problem isn't guest posting itself, it's where and how it's done. These are the patterns that turn a placement toxic:

  • PBNs and link farms. Networks of sites built only to sell links. They have no real audience, and Google is very good at spotting their footprints.
  • Fake or inflated metrics. A "DR 60" site with 50 visitors a month. The authority score was manufactured with spammy links, it carries no real weight.
  • Zero topical relevance. A finance link on a site that also posts about casinos, CBD, essay writing, and replica watches. Irrelevant neighbours drag you down.
  • Over-optimised anchors. Exact-match commercial anchors ("buy cheap car insurance") pointed at your money pages are one of the clearest spam signals there is.
  • Sites that openly sell links with visible footprints. "Write for us, $30, instant publish" pages with hundreds of unrelated sponsored posts.
  • Deindexed pages. If the article gets removed or the page falls out of Google's index, your link is worthless.
The core risk: a bad link profile built from cheap, irrelevant, or PBN placements can trigger Google's spam systems, and cleaning it up later (disavowing, removing links, waiting for re-crawls) costs far more time and money than doing it right the first time.

The 7-point vetting checklist

Before any site earns a placement, run it through these. If it fails two or more, walk away.

  1. Real organic traffic. Check the site in Ahrefs or Semrush. Steady organic traffic from Google is the single best sign it's legitimate. No traffic = no value, whatever the DR says.
  2. Traffic-to-DR sanity check. High DR with near-zero traffic is the classic fake-authority tell. The two should roughly match.
  3. Topical relevance. The site should genuinely cover your niche, not list it as one of forty unrelated categories.
  4. Clean outbound profile. Skim recent posts. If every article links out to gambling, crypto, or pharma with exact-match anchors, it's a link shop.
  5. Indexation. Search a few of the site's article titles in Google. If its pages don't show up, Google isn't counting them.
  6. Spam score. A quick Moz spam-score or a manual look at the backlink profile flags sites in bad neighbourhoods.
  7. Natural anchor + placement. Your link should sit inside the body of a relevant article with a natural anchor, not stuffed in an author bio or footer.

Keep the link profile natural

Even good sites can hurt you if you buy in the wrong pattern. Vary your anchor text (mostly branded and natural phrases, rarely exact-match), avoid buying 50 links in a week to a brand-new site, and mix guest posts with other link types. Slow and relevant beats fast and spammy every time.

What "safe" looks like

A safe guest post is simple: a real site, with real readers and real Google traffic, in or near your niche, publishing a genuinely useful article with one natural in-content link. That's the whole standard. Everything in the checklist above is just how you confirm it.
